# The story: how we made the provincial top 5 out of 200 teams

> **中文版本 → [STORY.zh-CN.md](./STORY.zh-CN.md)**

A record of the 7th Guangdong–Hong Kong–Macao Accounting Knowledge Competition. Result: out of 200 teams from 32 universities in Guangdong, our team placed in the provincial top 5 and advanced to the final on 2026-11-28. I was the team captain.

## Why I did it

I didn't treat this competition as a lottery ticket. I treated it as a checkpoint for my accounting foundations: rather than reading aimlessly, use a competition with a real deadline and real opponents to force systematic learning.

The problem was concrete: I was a first-year student with weak accounting fundamentals. How do you prepare for that?

## The action chain

### 1. Find real past papers, map the battlefield first

I found officially published question banks from previous editions online, as an xlsx file. I used AI to extract and classify the questions and answers, which produced the first key insight: the distribution was highly concentrated — enterprise and government accounting standards dominated.

The strategy shifted from "cover everything" to "break through the high-frequency core." That single decision shaped all time allocation afterwards.

### 2. The official explanations were too thin → write my own

The moment I started studying, the problem surfaced: official answers were one-liners. I couldn't understand them, let alone the reasoning behind them.

Instead of grinding through, I ran two tracks in parallel:

- Claude Code wrote per-question deep explanations and mnemonics in the background
- I watched junior-accounting course videos to build a framework and worked through 700 practice questions myself

One track built understanding, the other built foundations — both moving at the same time. I consider this the most cost-effective decision of the whole preparation.

### 3. Categories too coarse → tag every question by knowledge point

As explanations came in, I realized the initial classification was only broad categories. Within each category, knowledge points were jumbled, and revision kept jumping around.

So I had AI tag every question by knowledge point, cluster questions under the same tag, then count and sort tags by frequency — high-frequency exam points became visible at a glance, and my revision order became data-driven.

### 4. Still not clicking → upgrade from "drilling" to "teach first, then drill"

A few knowledge points still wouldn't stick. So I iterated the structure one more level: each module now opens with a focused explanation of the knowledge points (mnemonics + everyday analogies explaining the "why"), immediately followed by the corresponding questions. The documents went from "question bank" to "textbook you learn from before you drill."

### 5. The exam approached → split the material into two tracks

Time ran short. "Deep understanding" learning was no longer affordable in the final stretch, so I split everything in two:

| Track | Content | Usage |
|-------|---------|-------|
| Time-intensive | Modules that require working through problems to understand | Focus during regular prep |
| Cram-friendly | Pure memorization — recite it, score it | Intensive review before the exam |
| One-page sheet | Highest-frequency rules + numbers + educated-guess strategy | Final pass before walking in |

I also set an explicit abandonment strategy: hard but low-frequency topics like long-term equity investments and leases were skipped — under a time constraint, scoring beats completeness.

### 6. Personal output → team asset

As captain, I distributed everything to my teammates, so the whole team prepared from the same "high-frequency first + teach-then-drill + pre-exam cram" material instead of everyone flailing on their own.

## Result

Provincial top 5, advanced to the final.

Everything produced along the way is in this repo: module guides in `markdown/`, Word versions in `word/`, and the question data, scripts, and intermediate artifacts in `archive/`.
